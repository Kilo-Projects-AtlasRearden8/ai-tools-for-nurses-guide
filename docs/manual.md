## About this guide

This manual is for nurses and other healthcare workers who are curious about AI but have never used it, or who have tried it once and felt overwhelmed. No coding, no math, nothing to pay for. Every tool mentioned has a free option.

**Important:** This guide is for general education only. It is not medical advice, not a substitute for clinical judgment, and not a substitute for your facility's policies or required training. Always follow your employer's rules and scope of practice. Never paste real patient names, dates of birth, medical record numbers, photos, or any other patient information into an AI tool. When in doubt, leave patient details out.

## 1. Shift prep with AI

Use a free AI tool to get ready for your shift — reviewing conditions, medications, handoff questions, and your own prep checklist. Think of it as a study buddy that never gets tired, not as a clinical decision-maker.

### Before you start
- Open a free AI tool (see section 5).
- Have nothing patient-specific nearby that you would be tempted to paste.
- Keep your facility's policy on AI use in mind — some facilities restrict it entirely.

### Step 1: Pick your focus for the shift

Tell the AI what kind of shift you're working so the prep matches.

> "I'm a registered nurse working a 12-hour night shift on a medical-surgical floor. I'd like a short shift-prep checklist covering common conditions, meds to review, and handoff questions. Keep it in plain English and education-only — no patient data."

### Step 2: Review conditions you might see

Ask for plain-language refreshers on conditions common on your unit. The AI can outline typical signs, symptoms, and what nurses commonly watch for — you verify everything against your facility's references.

> "Give me a short, plain-English refresher on congestive heart failure: what it is, common signs and symptoms, and what nurses typically monitor. Use bullet points, about a 6th-grade reading level, education only."

### Step 3: Review medications

Ask about common medications on your unit: what they're for, typical side effects, and what nurses commonly monitor. Always double-check against your drug reference and pharmacy — AI can be wrong about doses.

> "I work med-surg. List 8 common medications on a med-surg floor, what each is used for, 2–3 common side effects to watch for, and one thing nurses commonly monitor. No doses, education only."

### Step 4: Practice handoff questions

Use AI to draft questions you might ask at shift handoff or that the oncoming nurse might ask you. Practice answering out loud.

> "List 8 good questions to ask during a nursing shift handoff on a med-surg unit, plus 4 questions the incoming nurse might ask me. Plain English."

### Step 5: Write a practice SBAR

SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) is a common handoff format. Ask the AI to show you an example structure — using a made-up scenario with no real patient details.

> "Show me a short example SBAR for a nurse calling a provider about a patient, using a completely made-up patient with no real names or dates. Label each section: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation. Education only."

### Step 6: Build your own checklist

Ask the AI to format everything into one short checklist you can screenshot, print, or copy into your notes app. Keep it generic — no unit-specific patient info.

> "Combine the above into a one-page shift-prep checklist for a med-surg night shift: conditions to review, medications to review, handoff questions, and a personal "before I leave home" list. Plain English, education only, no patient data."

### Red lines
- Never paste a real patient's name, room number, diagnosis, or any identifying details.
- AI outputs can be wrong about doses, indications, and clinical facts — always verify with your drug reference, facility policy, and clinical judgment.
- AI does not replace your assessment of a real patient.

## 2. Patient education with AI

Patients understand and remember more when instructions are short, plain, and focused on what to do. AI is good at rewriting clinical language into everyday language — about a 6th-grade reading level — so you can review it and use it as a starting point.

### Ground rules
- Never paste real patient details into an AI tool.
- Always review and sign off on anything before it reaches a patient.
- Prefer your facility's approved education materials first; use AI only to adapt or supplement.
- Use teach-back: ask the patient to explain it back in their own words.

### Step 1: Explain a condition in plain language

> "Explain type 2 diabetes in plain English for a patient who just received a new diagnosis. About a 6th-grade reading level. Use short sentences, bullet points, and include: what it is, common symptoms, and why treatment matters. Add a line saying this is general information and the patient should ask their care team about their own situation. Education only — not medical advice."

### Step 2: Draft discharge instructions

Ask the AI to organize discharge information into plain sections: medicines, warning signs, follow-up, and questions to ask. You fill in the patient-specific details yourself, on paper or in your facility's system — never in the AI tool.

> "Draft a plain-language discharge instruction outline for a patient going home after pneumonia. Sections: medicines, activity, warning signs to call about, follow-up appointments, and questions to ask the care team. About a 6th-grade reading level. Include a reminder to call 911 for emergency symptoms. Education only."

### Step 3: Draft medication teaching points

Draft short teaching points for common medications: what it's for, how to take it, common side effects, and when to call. Verify every detail against your drug reference before sharing.

> "Write 4 short teaching points for a patient starting lisinopril: what it does, how to take it, common side effects, and when to call the clinic. Plain English, no doses, education only."

### Step 4: Simplify existing instructions

Paste facility-approved text (no patient details) and ask the AI to make it shorter and simpler.

> "Rewrite the following wound-care instructions in plain English, about a 6th-grade reading level, using short sentences and bullet points. Keep all safety warnings. [paste approved facility text only, no patient details]"

### Step 5: Prepare teach-back questions

Ask the AI for questions you can use to check understanding without sounding like a test.

> "List 5 friendly teach-back questions a nurse can ask to check that a patient understands new medication instructions, without sounding like a test."

### Red lines
- Never paste patient names, photos, MRNs, or details that could identify someone.
- AI can invent facts — verify doses, side effects, and warnings against your drug reference and clinical judgment.
- Give patients your facility's approved materials whenever one exists.

## 3. Charting help with AI

AI can turn rough notes into tidy drafts, shorten wordy paragraphs, and show you how common note formats are structured. The key rule: you write from your own memory and observations, de-identified, and you review every line before anything goes in the chart.

### Golden rule: never paste patient data

Do not paste names, dates of birth, MRNs, room numbers, dates of service, photos, or any combination of details that could identify a patient. Work from de-identified bullets you wrote yourself:
- Instead of "Mr. Smith, room 412," write "patient A."
- Instead of "05/14/2026 at 1400," write "this afternoon."
- Keep it generic: "post-op day 2, abdominal incision."

### Step 1: Write your own bullets first

Jot down what you did and observed, on paper or in a private note — never in the AI tool with identifiers.

> Your notes: Patient A post-op day 2. Ambulated 3 times today with assist. Incision clean, dry, intact. Pain 3/10 after acetaminophen. IV site fine. Diet advanced to regular. Family present and taught about incision care.

### Step 2: Ask the AI to organize into a note format

> "Turn these de-identified nursing notes into a SOAP note (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan). Use plain professional nursing language. No patient names or dates. Education only. [paste de-identified bullets only]"

### Step 3: Choose a format your facility uses

Common formats: SOAP, DAP (Data, Assessment, Plan), SOAPIE (adds Intervention and Evaluation), and narrative. Ask the AI to show the structure so you can match your facility's template.

> "What is the difference between SOAP, DAP, and SOAPIE nursing notes? Give one short made-up example for each, no real patient details, education only."

### Step 4: Tighten wordy documentation

Ask the AI to shorten a paragraph you wrote while keeping all clinically relevant facts.

> "Shorten this nursing note paragraph but keep all clinically relevant facts. Professional tone, no fluff. [paste your own de-identified draft only]"

### Step 5: Review before anything goes in the chart

Read the AI draft line by line. Ask: Is it accurate? Is anything missing? Is anything added that didn't happen? Remove anything you didn't observe or do. Then enter it yourself in your facility's system.

### Red lines
- AI can hallucinate — it may invent vitals, medications, or events that never happened. Only you can verify what is true.
- Never let AI write directly into your EHR.
- Check your facility policy: some facilities prohibit AI in charting entirely. When in doubt, chart it yourself.

## 4. Personal scheduling with AI

Nursing schedules can be tough — rotating shifts, nights, weekends, overtime. AI can help you build your own personal plan: shift blocks, sleep around nights, and a week that leaves room for rest. You keep the schedule; AI only suggests.

### Step 1: List your fixed commitments

Before opening the AI tool, write down your knowns: work shifts, sleep needs, meals, commute, family time, appointments, and things that recharge you.

> Your list: Nights 19:00–07:30, Mon/Wed/Fri. Commute 30 minutes each way. Need 7–8 hours sleep. Kids' school pickup Tue/Thu 15:00. Grocery Saturday mornings.

### Step 2: Ask the AI to build a draft week

> "I work night shifts 19:00–07:30 on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, with a 30-minute commute each way. I need 7–8 hours of sleep and want time for meals, kids' school pickup Tuesday and Thursday at 15:00, and grocery shopping Saturday morning. Build me one sample week schedule in plain English, with sleep blocks around the night shifts. Education only — not a staffing tool."

### Step 3: Plan sleep around night shifts

Ask for a sleep plan that fits your rotation: anchor sleep, split sleep, or nap strategies — then adapt it to what actually works for your body.

> "Suggest a simple sleep schedule for a nurse working three 12-hour night shifts in a row, including when to sleep before the first shift and how to flip back to days after. Plain English, education only."

### Step 4: Block time for the things that matter

Use the AI to time-block your week: work, sleep, movement, rest, family, and nothing-time. Low-energy weeks are okay — the schedule serves you.

> "Turn the week above into a simple time-blocked schedule: work, sleep, commute, meals, one 20-minute walk per day, and one restful evening. Keep it low-pressure. Plain English."

### Step 5: Adjust for rotation changes

When your schedule changes, paste your new shift days (no patient or coworker details) and ask the AI to re-block the week.

### Red lines
- Never paste your roster, coworkers' names, shift patterns that identify patients or staff, or facility scheduling data.
- AI suggestions are not medical advice about sleep health — talk to your clinician if you have ongoing fatigue or sleep problems.
- Your manager's schedule and facility system always win.

## 5. Free AI models you can use today

You do not need to pay, and you do not need to be technical. These are free ways to try AI. Models change often, so treat this as a starting point, not a permanent list.

### A. Browser-based free options (easiest to start)
- Major AI companies usually offer a free tier you can use in a web browser or phone app. Look for "free" or "sign up with Google/Apple" options.
- Some let you use the model without creating an account at all.
- Trade-off: your conversations may be stored by the provider — read the privacy policy, and never paste patient data.

### B. Local-first options (runs on your device)
- **Ollama** (ollama.com) — free, open-source tool that runs models on your own computer. Nothing has to leave your device.
- **llama.cpp** and other open-source runners — free, for more technical users.
- Trade-off: you need a fairly modern computer, and setup takes a little time. Good if privacy matters most to you.

### Step-by-step: try a browser-based free model
1. Pick a well-known AI provider with a free web tier.
2. Create an account if required (use an email you don't mind sharing).
3. Start a new chat.
4. Copy one of the example prompts from this guide and paste it in.
5. Read the answer carefully — AI can be wrong.
6. When you're done, you can delete the chat in the provider's settings.

### Step-by-step: try a local-first model (Ollama)
1. Go to ollama.com and download the free app for your computer.
2. Install and open it.
3. Choose a small model to start (the site will suggest one).
4. Type a prompt in the chat window.
5. Because it runs locally, your conversation stays on your machine.

### Privacy checklist before you start
- [ ] No patient names, photos, MRNs, or identifying details — ever.
- [ ] I read the tool's privacy policy (or at least know it may store chats).
- [ ] My facility allows AI use for what I'm doing.
- [ ] I will verify anything clinical before relying on it.
- [ ] I won't enter passwords, credit cards, or other secrets.

### Red lines
- Free does not mean private. Assume anything you type could be seen by the provider. Never paste patient data.
- AI models can be confidently wrong about doses, symptoms, and clinical facts — verify everything against trusted references and your clinical judgment.

## Safety & privacy rules

**This is education only — not medical advice.** Nothing in this guide is medical advice, a diagnosis, a treatment recommendation, or a substitute for clinical judgment.

**Never paste patient data.** Never enter into an AI tool: patient names, photos, dates of birth, medical record numbers, room numbers, dates of service, diagnoses, or any combination of details that could identify someone.

**AI can be wrong.** Models can invent facts, doses, medications, and events. Always verify clinical information against your drug reference, facility policy, and clinical judgment.

**Before you use AI at work**
1. Read your facility's policy on AI and on personal device use.
2. Check whether AI in charting is allowed — many facilities prohibit it.
3. Use approved patient-education materials first.
4. Keep patient data out — always.
5. When in doubt, do it yourself without AI.
